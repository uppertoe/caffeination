How the score is calculated

Each option scores −log₂ of its share among the usual drinks it applies to. An option chosen by half the department scores 1 bit, one chosen by a quarter scores 2 bits, and one chosen by an eighth scores 3 bits.

A drink’s score is the sum of its options’ bits, so several slightly uncommon options can outweigh one rare one. Adding the bits treats the options as independent of one another, which the section on options that go together tests.

Each share includes an allowance of half a drink for every possible choice, so an option no one has chosen scores high rather than infinitely. The bits therefore differ slightly from the counts shown beside them.

How the estimate is made

The typical drink is the median of the department’s usual drinks. Small and regular milk drinks are counted as one espresso shot, and large milk drinks and long blacks as two. An extra shot adds one, and a weak drink takes off half. A café espresso shot averaged {{ shot_mg }} mg in Australian testing, though single shots have measured anywhere from {{ shot_range[0] }} to {{ shot_range[1] }} mg (Desbrow and colleagues, 2007).

How the simulation works

Each simulated shot’s caffeine is drawn from a gamma distribution with a mean of {{ shot_mg }} mg and a standard deviation of {{ sim.shot_sd }} mg. Those are the mean and standard deviation of 97 café espressos measured on the Gold Coast by Desbrow and colleagues in 2007. Drinks without espresso keep their fixed estimate.

In the first case, one draw sets the strength of every shot in the round. In the second, each drink draws its own. Errors that move together do not average out, which is why the first range is so much wider, and why a single café’s round is the realistic case.
