How the score is calculated

Each option scores −log₂ of its share among the usual drinks it applies to. An option chosen by half the department scores 1 bit, one chosen by a quarter scores 2 bits, and one chosen by an eighth scores 3 bits.

A drink’s score is the sum of its options’ bits, so several slightly uncommon options can outweigh one rare one. Adding the bits treats the options as independent of one another, which the section on options that go together tests.

Each share includes an allowance of half a drink for every possible choice, so an option no one has chosen scores high rather than infinitely. The bits therefore differ slightly from the counts shown beside them.

How the simulation works

Each simulated shot’s caffeine is drawn from a gamma distribution with a mean of {{ shot_mg }} mg and a standard deviation of {{ sim.shot_sd }} mg. Those are the mean and standard deviation of 97 café espressos measured on the Gold Coast by Desbrow and colleagues in 2007. Drinks without espresso keep their fixed estimate.

In the first case, one draw sets the strength of every shot in the round. In the second, each drink draws its own. Desbrow’s spread mixes differences between cafés with differences between shots at the same café, so the first range is an upper bound for a round from one café and the second a lower bound. Errors that move together do not average out, which is why the first range is so much wider.

Why the references disagree

AUSNUT builds every espresso drink from one laboratory-analysed espresso of 2,900 mg a litre, at 50 g a shot, which gives 145 mg a shot. Desbrow and colleagues measured 97 café espressos at an average of 2,473 mg a litre and 106 mg a serve, which implies a shot of about 43 mL. AUSNUT’s shot is both stronger and larger.

Both references are tables rather than measurements of the department’s actual cups, so the difference for each kind of drink is fixed by their recipes, and the analysis shows the pattern rather than measurement error. Bland and Altman introduced the plot in The Lancet in 1986 to compare clinical measurement methods, such as two monitors of the same variable. The limits of agreement show how far apart two methods might be for a single measurement, which is what matters when deciding whether one could replace the other.

How the time of day is summarised

Times of day wrap around midnight, so an ordinary average would place rounds at 11:30 pm and 12:30 am at midday. The circular mean treats each round as a direction on a clock face and averages the directions, which puts the department’s average round at {{ rounds.mean_time }}.

When rounds bunch in two places, such as mid-morning and mid-afternoon, the circular mean can fall in the quiet stretch between them, as an ordinary mean can between two peaks.

The mean resultant length, R = {{ '%.2f' % rounds.resultant }}, measures how tightly the rounds cluster: 0 for times spread round the clock, and 1 for every round at the same moment. The Rayleigh test asks whether times this clustered could arise if rounds were spread evenly round the clock, and gives {{ p_eq(rounds.rayleigh_p) }}. No one expects a round at 3 am, so passing that test is easy, and the test is shown for its method rather than its finding.

The shaded stretch is the shortest part of the day that holds half of all rounds. Unlike a mean and standard deviation, it stays honest when rounds bunch in two places, such as mid-morning and mid-afternoon.
