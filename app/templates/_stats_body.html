How unusual a drink is

{% if has_own %}The controls start on your usual drink.{% else %}The controls start on the department’s most common usual drink.{% endif %} Change any option to see how another drink compares.

{% with without_notes = true %}{% include "_drink_fields.html" %}{% endwith %}
How the score is calculated

Each option scores −log₂ of its share among the usual drinks it applies to, so an option chosen by half the department scores 1 bit and one chosen by an eighth scores 3 bits. A drink’s score is the sum of its options’ bits, which treats the options as independent of one another.

Each share includes an allowance of half a drink for every possible choice, so an option no one has chosen scores high rather than infinitely, and the bits differ slightly from the counts beside them.

How the estimate is made

The typical drink is the median of the department’s usual drinks. Small and regular milk drinks count as one espresso shot, and large milk drinks and long blacks as two. An extra shot adds one, and a weak drink takes off half. Each shot counts as {{ shot_mg }} mg, the average measured in Australian cafés, so every figure is an estimate rather than a measurement.

Randomisation and the baseline table

{{ pct(randomisation.tables_with_any) }} of a thousand random splits of the department into two arms show a “significant” difference at baseline, although every difference is chance.

Allocate the department again to draw another trial’s Table 1, and keep count of how often chance alone produces a p-value below 0.05.

Why baseline p-values mean nothing

When people are allocated at random, any baseline difference between the arms is chance by construction, so baseline p-values should spread evenly between 0 and 1. The CONSORT statement advises against testing them for this reason.

Carlisle turned the same fact around. In a 2017 analysis of more than 5,000 trials in anaesthetic and general medical journals, he looked for baseline tables whose p-values were too evenly matched, or too extreme, to have come from random allocation, and found trials whose data were later shown to be flawed or fabricated.
