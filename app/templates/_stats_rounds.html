How the time of day is summarised

Times of day wrap around midnight, so the shaded arc is the shortest stretch of the clock holding half of all rounds. Unlike a mean and standard deviation, it stays accurate when rounds bunch in two places, such as mid-morning and mid-afternoon.

The circular mean treats each round as a direction on the clock face and averages the directions, which puts the average round at {{ rounds.mean_time }}. When rounds bunch in two places, that mean can fall in the quiet stretch between them.

Each weekday’s bar carries a 95% Wilson interval, and a χ² goodness-of-fit test against an even spread across the days gives {{ p_eq(rounds.even_spread_p) }}.

How this was calculated

Each drink’s caffeine is its estimate in espresso shots, and guests’ drinks are left out. The first interval is Welch’s t interval, which treats every drink as independent. The second resamples colleagues 2,000 times, carrying all of each colleague’s drinks together, and takes the middle 95% of the resampled differences.

The Bayesian reading combines a normal prior centred on no difference with a normal likelihood built from the resampled interval. Narrowing the prior expresses more scepticism, so the posterior needs more data to move away from zero.

How this was calculated

Every gap between one colleague’s round and their next is a completed time. The gap since each colleague’s latest round is censored: their next coffee has not happened yet, so the log shows only that the gap is at least that long. Dropping those gaps, or treating them as finished, would bias the curve towards shorter gaps.

The Kaplan–Meier estimate keeps each censored gap in the count at risk until it is censored. The band uses Greenwood’s variance on the log(−log) scale. Each colleague contributes several gaps, so the band is somewhat narrower than it should be.

The rounds draw on the colleagues who come, so their mix of drinks can drift from the department’s. That drift is selection bias: a study of the rounds alone would describe the people who come for coffee, not the department.

How this was calculated

The Lorenz curve counts every active colleague, including those who have not yet been in a round, from those who come least often to those who come most. The Gini coefficient is twice the area between the curve and the diagonal of perfect equality, and its interval comes from resampling rounds 500 times. Guests are left out.

An average round holds {{ '%.1f' % z.mean }} colleagues’ drinks.

{% if z.after_big is not none %}

Rounds of {{ z.big }} drinks or more were followed by rounds averaging {{ '%.1f' % z.after_big }}, closer to the overall average, as chance alone predicts. A department that changed something after an unusually big round would see the next one shrink and credit the change, which is regression to the mean.

{% endif %}
How this was calculated

If each colleague joined each round independently at their own observed rate, round sizes would follow a Poisson-binomial distribution, whose variance on a fixed roster is smaller than its mean. The test compares the observed variance with that expected variance, two-sided, and gives {{ p_eq(z.ratio_p) }}.
