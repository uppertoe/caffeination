How this was calculated

Each drink’s caffeine is its estimate in espresso shots. The first interval is Welch’s t interval, which treats all {{ a.before + a.after }} drinks as independent. The second resamples colleagues with replacement 2,000 times, carrying all of each colleague’s drinks together, and takes the middle 95% of the resampled differences.

The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio pools the drink groups’ 2×2 tables with weights that favour the larger groups, and its interval uses the Robins–Breslow–Greenland variance. The Bayesian reading combines a normal prior centred on no difference with a normal likelihood built from the resampled interval. The interval on the area under the curve uses the Hanley–McNeil formula.

The curve shows the share of gaps in which the colleague has not yet had their next coffee, by days since their previous round, with a 95% band. The second row of numbers counts the gaps still at risk on each day, and the small ticks mark censored gaps. The dashed line marks one half.

How this was calculated

Every gap between one colleague’s round and their next is a completed time. The gap since each colleague’s latest round is censored: their next coffee has not happened yet, so the log shows only that the gap is at least that long. Dropping those gaps, or treating them as finished, would bias the curve towards shorter gaps.

The Kaplan–Meier estimate keeps each censored gap in the count at risk until it is censored, then drops it without counting an event. The band uses Greenwood’s variance on the log(−log) scale. Each colleague contributes several gaps, so the band is somewhat narrower than it should be, for the same reason as the drinks in the section above.

How this was calculated

The Lorenz curve counts every active colleague, including those who have not yet been in a round. The Gini interval comes from resampling colleagues 1,000 times. Spearman’s ρ ranks colleagues who have been in at least one round by how often they come and by the caffeine in their usual drink, then correlates the ranks. Its p-value comes from shuffling one ranking 999 times.

How this was calculated

If colleagues joined rounds independently, round sizes would follow a Poisson distribution, whose variance equals its mean. The dispersion test compares (n − 1) times the variance-to-mean ratio with a χ² distribution on n − 1 degrees of freedom.

The CUSUM chart adds up each round’s departure from the average, in standard deviations, less an allowance of half a standard deviation, and resets at zero. It signals when either sum passes 4 standard deviations.

The equivalence test runs two one-sided Welch t-tests, one against a difference of one drink in each direction, and reports the larger p-value.

How this was calculated

Fisher’s exact test adds up the probability of every table with the same row and column totals that is no more likely than the one observed. It needs no large-sample approximation, which matters with a handful of guests.

The fragility index is the number of guests who would have had to order the other way for the result to stop being significant. Critical care and anaesthesia trials often turn out to have a fragility index of only a few patients, which says more about a result’s robustness than its p-value does.
